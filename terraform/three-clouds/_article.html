01Licence and owners

Terraform is a compiler whose intermediate representation is a dependency graph and whose back end is somebody else's process. Parts 1, 2 and 3 read that compiler from the inside. This part puts it in the world. First the ecosystem: who owns which layer, how far the OpenTofu fork has drifted, and where the commercial features run. Then a single project that builds the same stack on AWS, GCP and Azure, taken through validate, mocked tests and a full plan with no cloud credentials at all. The part, and the collection, end with the register of all 101 findings.

The thesis of the part is that Terraform's open seams are the plugin protocol and the providers, and that almost everything else, including every new commercial engine, now runs outside the core binary. This section starts with the licence, because the licence draws the seam. Its claim: the files that stayed open are exactly the ones another tool needs to run the same providers.

One commit, one file

The relicensing is a single commit. On 2023-08-10 the hashicorp-copywrite[bot] account committed b145fbcaa, which replaced LICENSE with the Business Source License 1.1 and pointed to HashiCorp's announcement. The v1.5 branch had already been cut, so v1.5.7, tagged on 2023-09-07 from that branch, is the last MPL-2.0 release, and the last MPL commit on main is 8a085b427b74, the merge of #33590.

Three details in that grant narrow it. A "competitive offering" is a product "offered to third parties on a paid basis" that "significantly overlaps" with IBM's paid versions; a product that was not competitive when it first shipped does not become competitive when IBM adds features later; and "Products that are not provided on a paid basis are not competitive" (LICENSE:15-23). The change date is per version. The licence "applies separately for each version of the Licensed Work and the Change Date may vary for each version" (LICENSE:71-73). v1.6.0 was published on 2023-10-04, so by the four-year rule it becomes MPL 2.0 around October 2027; that date is arithmetic, not a statement in the file reconstruction. Each later release starts its own four-year clock.

For a practitioner the consequence is narrow. Running Terraform for your own infrastructure, in CI, or on an internal platform is untouched. Selling hosted Terraform runs that overlap HCP Terraform is what the grant excludes.

What stayed open

The interesting count is not the licence text but the SPDX headers. At 018a99c83c5d, 1,988 files carry SPDX-License-Identifier: BUSL-1.1 and 7 carry MPL-2.0 measured here (git grep over the tag). The seven are the two protocol definitions, docs/plugin-protocol/tfplugin5.proto and tfplugin6.proto; the four generated Go stubs for them under internal/tfplugin5 and internal/tfplugin6; and one Azure backend helper, internal/backend/remote-state/azure/helpers.go. The protocol directory has its own docs/plugin-protocol/LICENSE, which begins "Copyright IBM Corp. 2014, 2026" and then "Mozilla Public License, version 2.0" from the code.

Around the core, the libraries kept their licences. HCL and terraform-plugin-framework are MPL 2.0, now headed "Copyright IBM Corp."; go-cty, the value system from Part 1, is MIT under Martin Atkins's name; and each of the four cached provider packages ships an MPL 2.0 LICENSE.txt. HashiCorp's announcement said as much in advance: the BSL would cover "all future releases" of the products, while APIs, SDKs and almost all libraries would stay MPL 2.0 from the docs.

Put the two facts together and the shape is plain. A tool that wants to drive the existing provider ecosystem needs the wire protocol, the SDKs that providers are built on, and the provider binaries. All of those are MPL. Only the thing that reads .tf files, builds the graph and writes the state is BUSL. Part 2 showed the protocol is the stable seam between core and providers; the licence makes the same cut. OpenTofu runs the same provider binaries through it. Pulumi's terraform-bridge (Apache-2.0) wraps Terraform providers, and Crossplane's Upjet generates Crossplane providers from them, including the official AWS, Azure and GCP ones from the docs.

IBM, a year later in the tree

IBM agreed on 2024-04-24 to buy HashiCorp for $35 a share in cash, an enterprise value of $6.4 billion, and completed the purchase on 2025-02-27 from the docs. The repository caught up about a year later. Radek Simko's make copyrightfix commit 0fe906fa8 on 2026-02-13 switched the source headers to "Copyright IBM Corp.", and commit 4eba7c059 on 2026-03-11 changed the Licensor line from "HashiCorp, Inc." to "International Business Machines Corporation (IBM)", with a wording follow-up in f3658552ea six days later. Both first shipped in v1.15.0 on 2026-04-29 from the code.

The commercial surface moved too. The HCP Terraform legacy Free plan reached end of life on 2026-03-31, with organizations moved to an "enhanced" free tier of 500 managed resources. The pricing page is IBM-branded and charges per resource per month: $0.10 Essentials, $0.47 Standard, $0.99 Premium. HashiCorp support articles now redirect to ibm.com/mysupport from the docs.

The fork and the archive

The fork's side of the story is OpenTofu's own. The OpenTF manifesto asked HashiCorp to return Terraform to MPL or another well-known open-source licence. With no response, the fork was created on 2023-08-25, the repository went public on 2023-09-05, and the Linux Foundation announced OpenTofu on 2023-09-20 with 140-plus organizations, 600-plus individuals and at least 18 full-time developers pledged for five years. OpenTofu 1.6.0 went GA on 2024-01-10 as a "drop-in replacement", and the project entered the CNCF Sandbox on 2025-04-23. OpenTofu also reports a HashiCorp cease-and-desist letter of 2024-04-03 over its removed block, and published a response on 2024-04-11 arguing the code came from older MPL sources; HashiCorp's side of that exchange was not found from the docs.

One HashiCorp project did not survive the period. CDKTF, the CDK for Terraform, was sunset and archived on 2025-12-10 because it "did not find product-market fit at scale"; its last release was v0.21.0 on 2025-06-04, and the documented exit is cdktf synth --hcl, which writes readable HCL from the docs. If you still generate Terraform from TypeScript or Python with CDKTF, that command is the way out.

The licence, then, did not close the ecosystem. It closed the compiler and left the back-end contract open. The next section measures what a second compiler on that same contract has become.

02The fork, measured

OpenTofu began as Terraform 1.5 with a different licence. Three years on it is a different tool. It still loads .tf files, builds the same kind of graph and drives the same provider binaries, but its language, its state format and its CLI have each moved in directions Terraform did not follow, and Terraform has moved in directions it did not follow either. This section compares Terraform v1.17.0 with OpenTofu v1.13.2 (765062032c46, released 2026-10-21) with both binaries on the same box. Its claim: the two share the providers but not the newest RPCs, and two of OpenTofu's features are one-way doors.

The project, from its tree

The repository says what it is. LICENSE is MPL 2.0 with a dual copyright, the OpenTofu Authors and HashiCorp's from 2014, and forked files keep HashiCorp's MPL header under OpenTofu's. v1.13.x is supported until 1 August 2027. MAINTAINERS.md lists, among others, Martin Atkins, the go-cty author, and Spacelift's James Humphries from the code. The dependency pins have drifted: OpenTofu replaces hashicorp/hcl/v2 v2.20.1 with its own opentofu/hcl fork, while Terraform uses upstream v2.24.0, and OpenTofu is ahead on go-cty (v1.19.0 against v1.18.1) and go-plugin (v1.8.0 against v1.7.0). OpenTofu defaults to urfave/cli/v3 and keeps mitchellh/cli only behind TOFU_EXPERIMENTAL_CLI_ENABLED=false. It has a second execution engine under internal/engine, selectable only in nightly or dev builds through TOFU_X_EXPERIMENTAL_RUNTIME; releases still run the inherited graph engine. And it has no telemetry: Terraform's main.go:244-245 starts go runCheckpoint(ctx, config) on every run, which calls HashiCorp Checkpoint unless the CLI config disables it, and OpenTofu has no checkpoint code at all from the code.

Door two: encrypted state and plans

OpenTofu's terraform {} block accepts an encryption block. It holds labelled key_provider and method blocks plus three targets, state, plan and remote_state_data_sources, each with a method, an optional fallback for rotation, and enforced. v1.13.2 ships the key providers pbkdf2, aws_kms, gcp_kms, azure_vault, openbao and external (plus test-only ones) and the methods aes_gcm, unencrypted and external. pbkdf2 defaults to SHA-512, 600,000 iterations, a 32-byte salt and a 32-byte key from the code. The cipher is Go's GCM with a fresh nonce per write, opentofu:internal/encryption/method/aesgcm/aesgcm.go:25-45:

Smaller divergences, all measured

Version constraints. OpenTofu ignores required_version in a plain .tf file, on the assumption that it was written for "our predecessor", and enforces it only in .tofu files or through language { compatible_with }. With required_version = ">= 99.0.0" in main.tf, terraform init fails "Unsupported Terraform Core version" while tofu init and plan succeed; the same line in main.tofu makes tofu fail "Incompatible module" measured here.

Shadow files. When name.tf and name.tofu sit side by side, tofu silently ignores the .tf twin; the same holds for .tofu.json and .tofutest.hcl. With output "who" in both main.tf and main.tofu, tofu plans "tofu file", Terraform plans "tf file", and neither reports a duplicate. Code review that reads only .tf files can miss what tofu actually runs.

Distribution. A bare hashicorp/random resolves to registry.terraform.io in Terraform and registry.opentofu.org in tofu, and tofu rewrites in-memory state addresses from the first to the second unless the configuration uses the full form. tofu installs providers and modules from OCI registries; Terraform's oci backend is Oracle Object Storage, unrelated, and is the one backend tofu lacks. tofu takes a global flock per provider, version and platform beside the plugin cache, so parallel inits can share TF_PLUGIN_CACHE_DIR; Terraform has none, which bit this lab (section 8).

One protocol, two clients

Both trees carry the same 36 RPCs. OpenTofu's tfplugin6.10.proto defines everything in Terraform's 6.11 except the 6.11 computed-block and planned-private fields; as Part 2 showed, the labels no longer track the contents in either project. The difference is in the client. Terraform core calls 34 of the 36. OpenTofu's internal/plugin6/grpc_provider.go calls 21: schemas, validation, configure, the managed-resource and ephemeral RPCs and functions, and never the list-resource, action, state-store or GenerateResourceConfig RPCs, although their messages exist in its proto file. The CLI says the same thing from the outside: modules, query and stacks are Terraform-only commands, and the other 24 are common.

The direction of travel is visible in that gap. Every surface Terraform added since 1.13 (list resources and query, actions, state_store, Stacks, Policy) sits on an RPC or a plugin that OpenTofu does not call. OpenTofu's additions (encryption, provider iteration, -exclude, enabled, OCI) sit in the language, state and distribution, where Terraform cannot read them. The providers are the shared ground. Whether OpenTofu will add client support for the newer RPCs was not established.

03The commercial edge, and the ecosystem around it

Providers were always separate processes. What changed after 1.13 is that the commercial features joined them. Terraform Stacks and Terraform Policy are both reached from the CLI, and both run as plugins that core starts with go-plugin, the same machinery Part 2 took apart for providers. Neither engine is in the repository as an engine you can run locally. This section's claim: Terraform's direction is open core plus out-of-process commercial engines.

Stacks: a shim and a download

terraform stacks is a shim. The command's doc comment says it "delegates all execution to an internal plugin" (internal/command/stacks.go:34-35). The plugin is found by service discovery, stackspluginServiceID = "stacksplugin.v1" on defaultHostname = "app.terraform.io", reached over go-plugin with the cookie key TF_STACKS_MAGIC_COOKIE, and the command has its own exit codes: 98 if the plugin cannot be downloaded, 99 for an RPC error (stacks.go:46-75) from the code. The first terraform stacks -help, run with HOME pointed at a scratch directory, downloaded tfstacks 1.4.0, 22,585,528 bytes, into ~/.terraform.d/stacksplugin/, and printed sub-commands such as init, validate, create, list and fmt measured here. The downloaded binary was deleted afterwards.

The odd part is that the Stacks language and runtime are public. internal/stacks holds about 31,260 non-test Go lines in the BUSL tree, with stackconfig, stackplan, stackruntime and the .tfcomponent.hcl file type. A hidden rpcapi command bypasses the CLI's command package entirely; its README still calls the API experimental for the "Stacks private preview", although Stacks went GA on HCP Terraform in September 2025. The README is stale from the code.

The CLI never downloads the plugin. tfpolicy and tfpolicy-plugin 0.5.0 are separate releases on releases.hashicorp.com, each shipped with EULA.txt and TermsOfEvaluation.txt, and the plugin's help says it "executes Sentinel as an RPC API server" measured here (not re-run by a verifier). The protocol is small: Setup, EvaluateResource, EvaluateProvider, EvaluateModule, and a reverse CallbackService through which the engine can ask core for resources and data sources. It runs at three stages. At init, module- and provider-install hooks send each source and version to the engine and block the download on an error. In plan and apply, each instance's attrs and prior_attrs go to EvaluateResource from a separate policy subgraph whose nodes run even after their dependencies fail, under a TF_POLICY_PARALLELISM (or GOMAXPROCS) semaphore from the code.

Whether a failure stops the run depends on the stage. That asymmetry, and the entitlement check that makes local enforcement impossible with plugin 0.5.0, are measured on the project in section 7. Figure 23 puts the three kinds of plugin side by side.

What the big providers are made of

The providers on the shared seam are hybrids. The aws 6.70.0 and google 8.8.0 binaries link terraform-plugin-sdk/v2 v2.40.1 and terraform-plugin-framework v1.19.0, joined by terraform-plugin-mux v0.23.1, and azurerm 5.10.0's symbol paths show the same mix (framework 354 paths, sdk/v2 62, mux 34, plugin-go 73; built with go1.26.9); random 3.9.1 is framework-only measured here. HashiCorp's plugin docs send new providers to the framework and call SDKv2 the "legacy SDK"; mux exists so a provider can move from one to the other resource by resource from the docs. SDKv2 2.41.0 came out on 2026-10-06, so the three cached clouds are one SDK release behind.

On 2026-10-23 the Terraform Registry's v2 API reported 7,536 providers (34 official, 394 partner, 7,093 community, which sums to 7,521 for a reason not found) and about 24.9 thousand modules, 233 of them verified. hashicorp/aws showed about 8.1 billion downloads across 513 versions. The neighbours

Around Terraform sit tools that either reuse its providers or read its files from the docs. Pulumi (Apache-2.0) supports general-purpose languages and loads Terraform and OpenTofu providers through its bridge. Crossplane (Apache-2.0, graduated in CNCF on 2025-10-28) reconciles continuously in a cluster and gets providers from Upjet. CloudFormation is AWS-only and Bicep compiles to ARM JSON. Of the tools surveyed, only Terraform core is BUSL.

The stated direction matches the plugin picture. Terraform 1.14 added list resources, query and actions; HashiConf 2025 announced Stacks GA and public betas of search and actions in HCP Terraform; tfpolicy entered an HCP public beta on 2026-07-16 and went GA in the v1.17.0 CLI; HashiCorp's Terraform MCP server, an MPL-2.0 repository, was announced GA in June 2026. OpenTofu, for its part, says it plans to fold policy enforcement into its experimental built-in linting, inside the binary from the docs. HashiConf 2026 (26 to 29 October) falls after these sources were read.

04The multicloud project

The rest of this part is one project, ~/terraform-ref/lab/multicloud/, written for Terraform v1.17.0 and the four cached providers. It builds the same shape on each cloud: a network, private artifact storage, a container runtime, and a Postgres database whose password, in the README's words, "never touches state or plan files". It uses the newest language features on purpose, and it was taken as far as a machine with no cloud account can take it. Its claim: every one of those features can be exercised offline on all three clouds, except the ones that must talk to a cloud.

The shape

A root module calls three cloud modules. aws-stack builds a VPC with two private subnets, a security group, a versioned and public-access-blocked S3 bucket, RDS Postgres, a Secrets Manager secret, an IAM role and a container-image Lambda that triggers an action. gcp-stack builds a VPC with two subnets, a GCS bucket with public access prevention enforced, a service account, a Cloud Run v2 service, Cloud SQL Postgres with a google_sql_user, and a Secret Manager secret. azure-stack builds a resource group, a VNet with two delegated subnets (from a dynamic block), a ZRS storage account with TLS 1.2, a Log Analytics workspace, a Container Apps environment and app, a Postgres flexible server and an RBAC Key Vault with one secret. Around them: three test files, four mock-data directories, a .tfquery.hcl file, one policy file with its tests, and a fake az script.

Every module call fans out over environments. The password is per environment and cloud, built from a flattened key set (multicloud/main.tf:8-10): 

  db_keys = toset(flatten([
    for env in keys(var.environments) : [for c in local.clouds : "${env}-${c}"]
  ]))

and then, main.tf:17-36, an ephemeral resource over those keys and the first of the three module calls: 

ephemeral "random_password" "db" {
  for_each = local.db_keys

  length           = 28
  special          = true
  override_special = "-_~"
}

module "aws" {
  source   = "./modules/aws-stack"
  for_each = var.environments

  name                = "${var.project}-${each.key}${local.name_suffix}"
  cidr_block          = each.value.cidr_block
  db_size             = each.value.db_size

  db_password_version = var.db_password_version
  lambda_image_uri    = var.lambda_image_uri
  adopt_buckets       = var.adopt_buckets
}

var.environments is a map(object({ cidr_block = string, db_size = optional(string, "small") })) with two validation rules, one built on can(cidrnetmask(...)). local.name_suffix is "-invalid" whenever var.offline is true, so that no fake-credential run can name a real bucket, vault or account. gcp_project carries a cross-object rule (1.9) that reads another variable: var.offline || var.gcp_project != "xolab-offline-000000". The provider versions come from a local, which Terraform 1.17 allows in required_providers (#39153) as long as it is statically known, multicloud/versions.tf:3-19: 

locals {
  provider_pins = {
    aws     = "6.70.0"
    google  = "8.8.0"
    azurerm = "5.10.0"
    random  = "3.9.1"
  }
}

terraform {
  required_version = ">= 1.17.0"

  required_providers {
    aws = {
      source  = "hashicorp/aws"
      version = local.provider_pins.aws
    }

Only const variables and locals built from them or from literals work there. A plain variable fails init with "Only literal values and const variables can be evaluated during init." (Part 1 has the init graph that enforces it). The rest of the root holds two check blocks, three moved blocks recording the move from module.aws_dev to module.aws["dev"], and provider-defined functions on literals.

The schemas are the ground truth

Nothing in the project was written from memory of what a provider supports. It was checked against terraform providers schema -json for the cached versions, a 32.8 MB dump that a census script counted and a verifier re-ran byte for byte. Figure 24 is that census. Two of its rows decided the design. Every cloud has a write-only database password, so the same ephemeral pattern works on all three. And the list-resource coverage is uneven: google 8.8.0 has a list type for google_storage_bucket but none for google_compute_network, google_cloud_run_v2_service or google_sql_database_instance, which is why discover.tfquery.hcl lists buckets on GCP and resource groups on Azure.

What passes, and what it costs

All of the following ran on 2026-10-23 on the 2-vCPU box, from a shared plugin cache, with fake credentials where a provider demanded something measured here. Times vary with page cache and load, so they are ranges.

  • init passes in about 20 seconds and reports "Using … from the shared cache directory" for all four providers, but it is not offline: inside a network namespace with no network it fails "could not connect to registry.terraform.io".
  • validate takes 16 to 33 seconds, with one cold run at 1 minute 57; validate -query, which also checks the .tfquery.hcl file, takes 16 to 49 seconds and is not intrinsically slower. fmt -check -recursive passes, including test and query files.
  • terraform test: "Success! 7 passed, 0 failed." in about 42 to 65 seconds, every provider mocked (section 7).
  • terraform plan for one environment: "Plan: 36 to add, 0 to change, 0 to destroy. Actions: 1 to invoke." in about 35 seconds with the network up, under TF_LOG=DEBUG. Two environments plan 72 creates and 2 actions, 20.3 seconds on a warm run without debug logging. Fully air-gapped, the single-environment plan still succeeds and takes about 5 minutes 15 seconds (section 6 explains why).

The plan's costs are the ones Part 2 predicted. It started 18 provider processes, 5 each for aws, google and azurerm and 3 for random; each cloud provider was first started alone to serve its schema, which took about 7.2 seconds and about 580 MB for aws. At peak 7 providers were alive at once, about 1.38 GB of resident memory. The plan graph has 115 vertices and 217 edges, 84 of them (expand) nodes, plus 12 variables and 3 (validation) vertices, four providers and their four closers, three module closers, one ephemeral (close), two check (report) vertices, the action's (config) vertex and the root. The apply graph has 113 and 215; the default resource graph 37 and 39. There are still no instance nodes in it (Part 2). The saved plan is 28,866 to 28,900 bytes, and its show -json form 120,110 bytes, with a top-level action_invocations array beside resource_changes.

Functions that run offline, and one that surprises

Provider-defined functions run inside the provider process over CallFunction, on an unconfigured instance, so with literal arguments they evaluate at plan time without credentials. The root's function_demo output calls provider::aws::arn_parse, arn_build, provider::google::region_from_zone("us-central1-a"), which returns "us-central1", and provider::azurerm::parse_resource_id. Functions on computed attributes stay "(known after apply)" in a plan; under mocks they return real results because the mocks supply realistic ids.

One function does not do what its name suggests. provider::aws::trim_iam_role_path returns the role ARN with its path removed, not the role name; a test expecting "xolab-dev-api" failed, and the final assertion is "arn:aws:iam::123456789012:role/xolab-dev-invalid-api" measured here. If you need a role name, parse the ARN.

05Where the password goes

The project's most deliberate design choice is the database password. No variable holds it, no output shows it, and no file on disk should ever contain it. Part 3 read the mechanism, ephemeral values and write-only arguments, from the code. This section follows one password through a real three-cloud plan and checks every file it could have landed in. Its claim: the secret never touches disk, and the provider, not core, decides when it reaches the cloud.

One ephemeral value, six write-only arguments

Two releases built the pieces. v1.10 added ephemeral resources, which are opened anew in each phase and never persisted, together with ephemeral input variables and outputs; v1.11 (#36031) added write-only attributes, which a provider declares, which accept ephemeral values, and which are never stored from the code. The project's ephemeral "random_password" "db" makes one value per environment and cloud. Crossing into a module needs the child variable to be ephemeral too, as in modules/aws-stack/variables.tf:19-24: 

variable "db_password" {
  description = "Master password. Ephemeral: never persisted in state or plan."
  type        = string
  ephemeral   = true
  sensitive   = true
}

Inside each module the value goes to exactly two places: the database and a secret store. The AWS module's pair, modules/aws-stack/database.tf:25-43: 

  # Write-only argument (Terraform >= 1.11): sent to the provider on apply,
  # never stored in state or plan. Bump the version to push a new value.
  password_wo         = var.db_password
  password_wo_version = var.db_password_version
}

resource "aws_secretsmanager_secret" "db" {
  name = "${var.name}/db"
}

resource "aws_secretsmanager_secret_version" "db" {
  secret_id = aws_secretsmanager_secret.db.id
  secret_string_wo = jsonencode({
    host     = aws_db_instance.this.address
    username = aws_db_instance.this.username
    password = var.db_password
  })
  secret_string_wo_version = var.db_password_version
}

GCP uses google_sql_user.password_wo and google_secret_manager_secret_version.secret_data_wo; Azure uses administrator_password_wo on the flexible server and value_wo on the Key Vault secret. That is six write-only arguments fed from three ephemeral instances per environment. One comment in the project is slightly wrong: write-only values are not sent "on apply" only, as the next subsection shows.

The version triggers are not uniform. *_wo_version is a number on aws_db_instance and google_sql_user but a string on google_secret_manager_secret_version, so the GCP module converts it (modules/gcp-stack/main.tf:107-108): 

  secret_data_wo         = var.db_password
  secret_data_wo_version = tostring(var.db_password_version)

And write-only does not imply sensitive. aws's password_wo is both sensitive and write-only; azurerm's administrator_password_wo is only write-only measured here. The project marks the module variable sensitive = true anyway, which covers both.

The ephemeral resource itself

An ephemeral resource is not a resource with a hidden value; it has its own lifecycle in the graph. ephemeralResourceOpen (internal/terraform/node_resource_ephemeral.go:90-118, 140-189) first looks at the configuration. If it is not wholly known, core makes a synthetic unknown, marks it ephemeral and calls no provider at all. Otherwise it calls ValidateEphemeralResourceConfig and then OpenEphemeralResource, and marks the whole result with marks.Ephemeral from the code. Each ephemeral configuration resource also gets a (close) vertex in the plan graph, so its instances are closed as soon as nothing needs them; the project's plan graph has exactly one. One apply of a configuration like this makes two OpenEphemeralResource and two CloseEphemeralResource calls, one pair per walk measured here.

Two neighbours behave differently and are worth knowing before you reuse the pattern. Since v1.16, terraform_data has a store block whose write-only store.input accepts an ephemeral value and copies it into sensitive_output, which is persisted: stored with sensitive = true, a random_password result lands in state. It is a durability tool, not a secrecy tool. And a test assert cannot read an ephemeral resource at all, mocked or not ("Ephemeral resources cannot be asserted"); the project's mock simply returns "mock-password-not-secret".

What the plan shows, and what the files hold

The offline plan opens the ephemeral instances and renders the argument without a value measured here, trimmed: 

ephemeral.random_password.db["dev-aws"]: Opening...
ephemeral.random_password.db["dev-aws"]: Opening complete after 0s
  # module.aws["dev"].aws_db_instance.this will be created
      + password_wo                           = (write-only attribute)
      + password_wo_version                   = 1

The renderer has a second form, (sensitive, write-only attribute) (internal/command/jsonformat/computed/renderers/write_only.go:24-28), but aws's sensitive password_wo still printed the plain one. In show -json, every *_wo is null and every *_wo_version holds its configured value: the six arguments, plus an unused pair on azurerm_subnet.

A null in the JSON view does not prove the bytes are absent from the plan file. A canary does. A one-resource configuration fed an ephemeral root variable into password_wo and was planned with a recognisable value measured here: 

$ TF_VAR_pw=CANARY-7f3a-SECRET $T plan -out=wo6.tfplan    # Plan: 1 to add
$ python3 -m zipfile -e wo6.tfplan unz6 && grep -a -c CANARY unz6/tfplan
0
$ python3 -c "d=open('unz6/tfplan','rb').read(); print(d[-12:])"
b'\xc8\x01\x01\xd0\x01\x01\xe2\x01\x02pw'     # field 28 apply_time_variables = ["pw"]

The canary is in none of the ZIP's members: not tfplan, tfstate, tfstate-prev, tfconfig/* or the lock file. A verifier repeated it with a fresh canary, CANARY-d9e1-SECRET, and got the same result. What the plan does keep is the variable's name. internal/plans/plan.go:58-67 from the code: 

	// ApplyTimeVariables retains the names of any ephemeral variables that were
	// set (non-null) during the planning phase and must therefore be
	// re-supplied by the caller (potentially with different values) during
	// the apply phase. Ephemeral input variables are intended for populating
	// arguments for other ephemeral objects in the configuration, such as
	// provider configurations. Although the values for these variables can
	// change between plan and apply, their "nullness" may not.
	VariableValues     map[string]DynamicValue
	VariableMarks      map[string][]cty.PathValueMarks
	ApplyTimeVariables collections.Set[string]

Those are the trailing bytes above: tag e2 01 is field 28, length 2, then pw. The show -json view lists only the non-ephemeral variable, {'mode': {'value': 'wo'}}. The practical consequence: to apply a saved plan you must pass the ephemeral variables again, and an ephemeral resource such as the project's random_password is reopened at apply, so the password applied is a fresh random value, not the one that existed during plan.

Try to put the value somewhere persistent and core refuses before any provider call: feeding it into an ordinary password argument fails "Invalid use of ephemeral value … not a write-only attribute and must be persisted to state." Figure 25 maps where the ephemeral mark may go.

Core validates; it does not scrub

The mechanism behind the nulls is a check, not a filter. After PlanResourceChange, core runs internal/terraform/node_resource_abstract_instance.go:1081-1092 from the code: 

		// Providers are supposed to return null values for all write-only attributes
		writeOnlyDiags := ephemeral.ValidateWriteOnlyAttributes(
			"Provider produced invalid plan",
			func(path cty.Path) string {
				return fmt.Sprintf(
					"Provider %q returned a value for the write-only attribute \"%s%s\" during planning. Write-only attributes cannot be read back from the provider. This is a bug in the provider, which should be reported in the provider's own issue tracker.",
					n.ResolvedProvider, n.Addr, tfdiags.FormatCtyPath(path),
				)
			},
			plannedNewVal,
			schema.Body,
		)

The same check runs at refresh, apply and import. Core nulls write-only attributes itself only where it fabricates an object: test overrides, mock providers and Stacks stubs. That is internal/lang/ephemeral/strip.go:29-41, which keeps the marks on the null it substitutes: 

func (w *writeOnlyTransformer) Enter(path cty.Path, value cty.Value) (cty.Value, error) {
	attr := w.schema.AttributeByPath(path)
	if attr == nil {
		return value, nil
	}

	if attr.WriteOnly {
		value, marks := value.Unmark()
		return cty.NullVal(value.Type()).WithMarks(marks), nil
	}

	return value, nil
}

So "write-only" does not mean "sent once". Core sends the value in the configuration of every PlanResourceChange and ApplyResourceChange and demands only that it not come back. The provider decides when to act on it: at create, or when the *_wo_version changes. That is why the version argument exists, and why rotating means bumping db_password_version, not changing the password.

That design has one caveat for this project, which is reasoning and was not tested reconstruction. The ephemeral random_password is different on every run, and providers push a write-only value only when its version changes. If the database or the secret is ever recreated without a version bump, the two can silently hold different passwords, and nothing outside Terraform recorded either one except the secret store. If you copy this pattern, treat the secret store as the only record and bump the version whenever either side is replaced.

06Planning without credentials

A plan of an empty state has nothing to refresh, so in principle it needs no cloud at all. In practice every provider runs ConfigureProvider first, and what each one does there decides whether a credential-free plan is possible. The usual advice is "fake credentials plus skip flags". On these three clouds that advice is three different stories, and on one of them it does not work. This section measures each provider alone, then the full offline plan.

The probe

Each provider was planned on its own with no credentials and every ambient identity source blocked: AWS_EC2_METADATA_DISABLED=true, the GCE metadata server pointed at the closed port 127.0.0.1:9, and no az on PATH measured here.

  • aws 6.70.0 refuses. ConfigureProvider fails "No valid credential sources found", exit 1, after 9.5 seconds.
  • google 8.8.0 plans. Exit 0 after 13.9 seconds. Its authentication is lazy: it logs "Authenticating using DefaultClient", fails the universe-domain lookup, and fails the userinfo lookup locally, because the token fetch from the blocked metadata server is refused. No request reaches Google in this mode, and the plan never needs an API call.
  • azurerm 5.10.0 refuses. It must build an authorizer during configure, and with nothing else available it tries the CLI: exec: "az": executable file not found in $PATH, exit 1, after 2.3 seconds.
  • random 3.9.1 needs nothing.

AWS: a fake key and four switches

AWS is the easy case. The project's providers.tf:7-15 gives it a placeholder key pair and turns on every skip the provider offers when var.offline is true: 

provider "aws" {
  region = var.aws_region

  access_key                  = var.offline ? "AKIAOFFLINEOFFLINE00" : null
  secret_key                  = var.offline ? "offline-secret-key-not-real" : null
  skip_credentials_validation = var.offline
  skip_requesting_account_id  = var.offline
  skip_metadata_api_check     = var.offline
  skip_region_validation      = var.offline

With those four switches the provider never calls STS or the instance metadata service, and the TF_LOG=DEBUG log of the full plan has zero HTTP Request Sent lines for aws. The two data sources the module uses, aws_region and aws_iam_policy_document, compute locally measured here. This shows the set is sufficient, not that every switch is necessary. One caution comes from the lab's own history: an earlier run with a plausible bucket name reached a real bucket, which is why every globally unique name now ends in -invalid when offline is set. Fake credentials do not stop a provider from sending a request; they only make it fail.

Google: one real call, and five minutes without a network

Google would plan with nothing at all, but the project gives it access_token = "offline-token-not-real" so the provider skips its metadata probe. That mode has a cost. During ConfigureProvider the provider makes one real HTTPS request to openidconnect.googleapis.com/v1/userinfo with the fake token. Google answers 401 "Invalid Credentials"; the provider logs it at INFO, carries on with an empty identity, and the plan succeeds. The six www.googleapis.com URLs elsewhere in the log are OAuth scope strings, not requests measured here.

Take the network away and the same request becomes expensive. Run inside a network namespace with no network (unshare -rn), the full plan still succeeds with the same 36 creates and 1 action, but takes about 5 minutes 15 seconds instead of about 35: google retries the userinfo call about 68 times, logging "Dismissed an error as retryable", before it gives up. The provider work itself is about 15 seconds of that. No supported switch to suppress the call was found. If you plan in an air-gapped CI runner with the google provider, that retry loop is where the time goes.

Azure: Entra ID says no, so fake the CLI

azurerm cannot configure without a token. The obvious fake, client-secret authentication with all-zero ids, is not offline: the provider sends the ids to the Microsoft Entra ID token endpoint, which rejects them with HTTP 400 and "AADSTS700038: 00000000-0000-0000-0000-000000000000 is not a valid application identifier" measured here. So the project uses CLI authentication against a fake az, providers.tf:36-49: 

provider "azurerm" {
  features {}

  subscription_id                 = var.azure_subscription_id
  tenant_id                       = var.azure_tenant_id
  resource_provider_registrations = "none"

  # azurerm cannot be configured without a token: client-secret auth with fake
  # ids is rejected by Entra ID (AADSTS700038). Offline we use CLI auth against
  # tools/fakeaz/az (an unsigned placeholder token, put it first on PATH);
  # online we use OIDC workload identity federation from CI.
  use_cli  = var.offline
  use_oidc = !var.offline
}

tools/fakeaz/az is a 21-line shell script. It answers exactly four commands, az version, az account show, az account list and az account get-access-token, the last with a JWT whose header is {"alg":"none","typ":"JWT"}, and exits 1 on anything else. Its own comment says it "grants nothing: any real ARM call with it fails with 401". During the plan the provider called it exactly four times, the last time asking for a Microsoft Graph token (--scope https://graph.microsoft.com/.default), and made no network calls. The first version of the shim lacked account list, and configure failed until it was added measured here.

The audit, and what cannot be faked

Put together, the offline plan's traffic is small and fully accounted for. aws sends nothing: no STS, no IMDS, no API call, because state is empty and nothing refreshes. google sends one HTTPS request and gets a 401. azurerm runs four local az commands. random does nothing. Re-running the plan with no network at all confirmed it: only google attempted a connection measured here.

terraform query is the command no fake can rescue. A list block calls the provider's ListResource RPC, which makes live API calls by design. With the aws s3 and sts endpoints pointed at the closed port, the run logged nine HTTP Request Sent … tf_rpc=ListResource lines, turned limit = 5 into max-buckets=5 on S3 ListBuckets, and failed after 58 seconds with "exceeded maximum number of attempts, 9". In another lab, a query against the real AWS endpoint with fake keys got "401 … AuthFailure" and wrote no configuration. Offline, only terraform validate -query is available, and it checks the file, not the cloud. Part 3 covers what query does with real credentials.

The general lesson is about where authentication happens. aws checks credentials only if asked to, google checks them lazily and tolerates failure, and azurerm cannot build a client without a token. None of that is in Terraform core; it is each provider's ConfigureProvider, one RPC from Part 2's table, doing what its SDK and authors chose.

07Tests and policy on the project

Two features promise to check a configuration before anything is applied: terraform test with mock providers, and Terraform Policy. Both ran on the project with no credentials. Both did something, and neither did quite what its name suggests. The claim of this section: mocks are not isolation, and local policy is not enforcement.

The suite

Seven runs in three files. tests/plan.tftest.hcl has five plan-only runs: the default shapes, a two-environment fan-out asserting two instances of each cloud module, a bad CIDR and an online-mode project id that must fail validation (expect_failures = [var.environments] and [var.gcp_project]), and two environments with the same CIDR, which must only warn (expect_failures = [check.environment_cidrs_disjoint] passes because a failing check is a warning). tests/apply.tftest.hcl runs one mocked apply of all three clouds and asserts on provider-function outputs. tests/override.tftest.hcl holds one run with an override_resource. Policy offline, and policy in plan

policies/storage.policy.hcl has one rule per cloud. The AWS rule, lines 22-27: 

resource_policy "aws_s3_bucket_public_access_block" "all_blocked" {
  enforce {
    condition     = attrs.block_public_acls && attrs.block_public_policy && attrs.ignore_public_acls && attrs.restrict_public_buckets
    error_message = "S3 public access block must enable all four switches."
  }
}

The language is HCL with its own blocks: a policy block with its own required_providers, then resource_policy and module_policy blocks whose enforce blocks pair a condition with an error_message, plus filter, enforcement_level, input and locals; a rule reads the planned values through attrs, the prior ones through prior_attrs, and metadata through meta from the docs. The project's Azure rule is marked enforcement_level = "advisory", the GCP rule has two enforce blocks.

The standalone tfpolicy CLI evaluates that offline against its own tests, and the same policy handed to terraform plan is not evaluated at all measured here:

The six policy tests (three pass, three expected failures) took 12.9 to 24.2 seconds. --policies and --tests must name directories; the docs' single-file form failed "not a directory". The first runs installed both the lowest and the highest provider version matching each constraint, about 2.2 GB, after which a 17 MB schema cache sufficed. A verifier did not re-run the tfpolicy side; the plan side is confirmed from the recorded run.

The plan result has two causes. The fail-open is deliberate, internal/command/plan.go:106-116: 

	if len(args.PolicyPaths) > 0 {
		client, policyDiags, stopClient := c.PolicyClient(c.CommandContext(), args.PolicyPaths, backendPolicyEntitlement(be, policy.PlanEvaluationStage))
		// if there has been any errors when setting up the policy client, we log them but
		// we still proceed with the operation, as a failure to set up the policy client
		// should not prevent the plan operation from running
		if opReq.View != nil && policyDiags != nil {
			opReq.View.PolicyDiagnostics(policyDiags)
		}
		opReq.PolicyClient = client
		defer stopClient()
	}

apply behaves the same, while init -policies returns 1 on the same setup diagnostics (internal/command/init_run.go:294-299). And the setup fails because a local backend supplies no entitlement. Core's comment on the field says "Nil means the plugin skips the check" (internal/policy/policy.go:71-74); tfpolicy-plugin 0.5.0 does not skip it. Real enforcement appears to need a cloud or remote backend with an entitled organization; that is inferred from backendPolicyEntitlement, not measured. Whether any plugin version honours the nil comment is open; only 0.5.0 was tested.

The practical rule: in CI, a zero exit code from plan -policies proves nothing about policy. Run tfpolicy test as its own step, or check the output for "Failed to setup Terraform Policy connection". Figure 23 shows where the plugin sits, and Part 3 traces the GA entry and its PR-number mismatch.

08What was not run, and the register

Everything so far stopped before a cloud. That was the constraint, not a choice: no AWS, GCP, Azure or HCP credentials existed at any point. So the project's last chapter is a checklist that was written and never executed. It is worth reading for what it says about where the remaining risk sits. The claim of this section: the hard parts left are identity and networking, and no plan can test either.

The real-apply checklist, unexecuted

Setting -var offline=false is the switch. Every fake credential in providers.tf collapses to null, so each provider falls back to its normal credential chain, azurerm switches to use_oidc = true, and the gcp_project validation then demands a real project id. Only that last step was measured, by the test that expects the validation to fail. The rest comes from the project README and provider documentation, and none of it was run or verified here reconstruction:

  • All clouds. A remote backend with locking: S3 with use_lockfile = true, GCS, or azurerm blob leases (Part 3 shows why the S3 switch matters). HCP Terraform is the only route found to real -policies enforcement, and that is inferred from code. When applying a saved plan, re-supply the ephemeral input variables.
  • AWS. CI authentication through GitHub Actions OIDC into a deploy role with sts:AssumeRoleWithWebIdentity; permissions for VPC, S3, RDS, Secrets Manager, IAM and Lambda; a real ECR image instead of the placeholder URI; globally unique bucket names. The Lambda runs in two private subnets with no NAT gateway and no VPC endpoint, so at runtime it cannot reach ECR, Secrets Manager or STS until one is added. Whether a Lambda function error, as opposed to an API error, fails the smoke action and so halts under the default on_failure is unknown.
  • GCP. Workload Identity Federation instead of JSON keys; six APIs enabled (compute, sqladmin, run, secretmanager, iam, servicenetworking); roles/secretmanager.secretAccessor for the Cloud Run service account. Cloud SQL with a private IP needs a private services access range and a google_service_networking_connection, which the module does not model, so a real apply would fail at the SQL instance as written.
  • Azure. OIDC workload identity federation with ARM_CLIENT_ID, ARM_TENANT_ID and ARM_SUBSCRIPTION_ID; real ids in place of the all-zero defaults; the resource providers registered, since the project sets resource_provider_registrations = "none"; Key Vault Secrets Officer for the deploy identity. Purge protection leaves a destroyed vault soft-deleted. The flexible server sits on a delegated subnet with public access off but no private_dns_zone_id, which Azure is believed to require for VNet-integrated servers; the schema says only optional and computed. Whether the /23 Container Apps subnet matches the environment type Azure creates was not checked.

Read the list by kind. Every cloud's first item is an identity federation set-up, and most of the rest is network plumbing: private DNS, private service access, NAT or endpoints, delegated subnets. A plan cannot catch any of it. It checks schemas, types and the graph; it does not ask a cloud whether a subnet can reach a registry or whether a role may assume another. Those are the failures that would appear first on a real apply, and they are the ones this lab could not reach.

Hazards found while building

The one class of failure the lab did hit was local: the shared plugin cache. Terraform has no lock on TF_PLUGIN_CACHE_DIR. Concurrent init runs, including the lab's own credential probe, rewrote all four cached providers twice within about five minutes, and a plan that ran during the rewrite failed its lock-file checksum check. One init that coincided with a rewrite downloaded aws again, about 830 MB, despite a pinned lock file; afterwards all four entries matched the lock's h1: hashes. A verifier's no-lock init in another lab failed "text file busy" because a different process was running the binary it tried to replace measured here. OpenTofu's per-package flock exists for exactly this case (section 2). If several jobs share one cache directory, serialise their init steps.

The thesis, once more

Terraform is a compiler whose intermediate representation is a dependency graph and whose back end is somebody else's process. The four parts followed that sentence from bytes to state. Part 1 showed a parser that builds structure without meaning and a decoder that runs twice. Part 2 showed a graph that is the program and providers that are untrusted processes behind a 36-RPC contract. Part 3 showed a single plaintext document held together by advisory locks, and a decade of features that each arrived as a small schema, a graph node and an RPC. This part showed the same seams from outside. The licence leaves the protocol and the providers open. The fork shares them and diverges everywhere else. The commercial engines run as separate plugins. And a three-cloud project can be validated, tested and planned without a single credential, up to the point where a cloud must answer.
