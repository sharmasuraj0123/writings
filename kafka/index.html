Kafka, taken apart

Kafka is one data structure used five times: an append-only log whose disk format is its wire format, replicated by followers pulling. Nearly every promise it makes is a statement about where a line sits in such a log, not about a disk or an outcome: the high watermark, the last stable offset, the recovery point, the committed offset, the metadata commit. Read a guarantee as "which line, in which log, moved by whom", and both the guarantees and their edges follow.

Five parts, read in order or alone. Every claim cites a file and line at Apache Kafka 4.4.0 (kafka@1156b275), a client source (librdkafka v2.16.0, franz-go at 123ec885), a KIP, or a measurement. Real brokers ran on one 2-vCPU machine over loopback, so timings show relative behaviour and are never capacity. No power cut was run: all-replica loss and flipped bytes are simulated damage, and are labelled so. Each part is one self-contained HTML file.

The collection

  • Part 1·6 November 2026·37 min read

    The log on disk

    A partition is a directory of segment files holding batches exactly as the producer sent them. This first part of a five-part teardown reads Apache Kafka 4.4.0 down to the byte: the 61-byte batch header and what its CRC leaves out, an append that fsyncs nothing by default, two sparse indexes nobody checksums, rolls and retention driven by record timestamps, a tombstone with two clocks, and what recovery does to hand-injected damage, from one lost batch to a broker that will not start.

  • Part 2·6 November 2026·35 min read

    One request, on the wire and through the broker

    A broker is two thread pools and one queue, and one rule, the muted socket, gives every connection one request at a time. This second part of a five-part teardown follows a request through Apache Kafka 4.4.0: a real 139-byte Produce decoded by hand, a batch that reaches the consumer changed only in a field the broker stamps, requests parked in purgatory holding no thread, a Produce born with its timeout error already set, the timing wheel underneath, quotas that answer first and go silent after, a protocol generated from 203 JSON files, and a Produce version the broker advertises and refuses on purpose.

  • Part 3·6 November 2026·36 min read

    What an ack promises: replication and the metadata quorum

    An acks=all ack says a record reached the page cache of every in-sync replica and that the in-sync set met a floor. It says nothing about disk. This third part of a five-part teardown reads Apache Kafka 4.4.0 line by line: followers that ack by asking for the next offset, a high watermark that is a minimum, two detectors that decide who leaves the set, a frozen leader that acks what it later deletes, the election ladder down to a last known leader that KIP-966 intends, and the fsyncing metadata log whose loss freezes every one of those lines. All-replica loss here is simulated damage on one host, and its numbers are set by the mock.

  • Part 4·6 November 2026·32 min read

    Producers and consumer groups

    A producer's guarantees stop at sessions and retries, and a consumer's position says what was handed over, not what was finished. This fourth part of a five-part teardown reads the clients of Apache Kafka 4.4.0: two threads around a queue, what linger really decides, a key hash that differs by client, the broker state idempotence leans on and the places it runs out, a commit decoded byte by byte, the classic barrier against KIP-848's heartbeat, and share groups that replace ownership with leases.

  • Part 5·6 November 2026·45 min read

    Exactly-once, as it is

    A Kafka transaction is two logs agreeing after the fact: a decision in one, markers in others, a stall in between. This last part of a five-part teardown reads Apache Kafka 4.4.0 and takes one apart byte by byte: the coordinator's state machine, Transaction Version 2, a 78-byte marker, the last stable offset that stalls plain records too, the epoch fence, and KAFKA-20734, known upstream, where a marker for an offline partition is dropped and the coordinator says complete. That bug has no tagged fix as of 6 November 2026. The part ends where exactly-once ends, and with the whole collection's findings register.
