{{S}}The timing wheel

Every parked request needs a timeout, and most never fire. The docs for the structure give that as its reason: "The hierarchical timing wheels works especially well when operations are completed before they time out" (server-common/…/server/util/timer/TimingWheel.java:89). So cancel has to be cheap, and insert nearly so. A hierarchical timing wheel gives O(m) insert for m wheels and O(1) cancel from the code.

The default timer has a 1 ms tick and 20 buckets per wheel (SystemTimer.java:44-46). Each overflow wheel's tick is the previous wheel's whole span. Spans and ticks are derived from those two numbers:

Overflow wheels are created lazily, the first time an entry does not fit. The class comment explains the contrast it is drawing: priority-queue timers "have O(log n) insert/delete cost", and a simple wheel cannot hold a timeout past its own span, which is "an overflow". The elided branches refuse an entry that is cancelled or already expired. Note what goes into the DelayQueue: queue.offer(bucket). It holds buckets, not tasks, and a bucket enters only when its expiry changes. A heap costs log n per item, and here n counts active buckets rather than requests. That is why a broker can park a great many requests cheaply.

Walk the figure in terms of the code. An entry due at 517 ms arrives with the clock at 0. It does not fit under 20 or under 400, so add recurses twice and lands in the 400 ms wheel, in bucket 1, whose expiry is 400. Nothing happens for 400 ms. Then the bucket expires, the entry is re-added, and 517 now fits in the 20 ms wheel, in bucket 5. At 500 it drops into the 1 ms wheel, in bucket 17. At 517, add finds it already expired and returns false, and only then does the task run. That is one placement and two moves over its whole life, and if the request completes first, the moves never happen.

The clock moves only when a bucket expires. SystemTimer.java:89-101 polls the queue, advances the clock to that bucket's expiry, and flushes the bucket by re-adding each entry. That re-add either drops the entry to a finer wheel or runs it. A read/write lock excludes adds during the tick from the code.

One number here gets misread. The reaper calls advanceClock(200L) (DelayedOperationPurgatory.java:409-421). That is not a 200 ms tick. It is the longest the poll idles, because DelayQueue.poll returns as soon as the earliest bucket expires. The lab's 6,000 ms wait took 6,005 ms, in a single run on loopback measured here.

ReplicaManager owns six purgatories at the pin, and the live thread dump shows a reaper for each: Produce, Fetch, DeleteRecords, RemoteFetch, RemoteListOffsets, ShareFetch. Two more ExpirationReaper-1-AlterAcls threads come from outside it measured here.
