{{S}}Forks and truncation

Replicas diverge when leadership moves. Kafka's answer is a number stamped in every batch: the leader epoch. Each replica rebuilds its epoch-to-start-offset map from those stamps as it appends (UnifiedLog.java:1226-1229). A new leader records its epoch's start offset as its current LEO, and never truncates itself: everything up to its LEO becomes authoritative, including records never committed under the old leader (Partition.scala:636-638, :649) from the code.

A follower of a new leader does not truncate first either. It keeps fetching from its LEO and carries its last epoch, and the leader decides. If that epoch does not match the leader's cache, the leader returns an empty read plus a diverging-epoch hint (:1401-1408). A diverging fetch does not count as progress (:1313). An epoch's end offset is the start of the next higher epoch the leader knows (LeaderEpochFileCache.java:297). The follower truncates to the lower of the two end offsets, or to an intermediate point if it does not know that epoch, and asks again (AbstractFetcherThread.scala:634-641). The truncating fetch applies no data, so the follower never exposes a HW over bytes it is about to delete (:357-359). Purgatory does not delay any of it: a parked follower fetch completes as soon as divergence is seen (DelayedFetch.scala:109).

Every epoch-bearing read also compares epochs: an older request is fenced, a newer one told to retry (Partition.scala:375-378). A deposed leader finds out on its next request. That sentence is the whole problem, as a frozen leader shows.

Under acks=all, every acked value came back, and 49 in-flight sends failed with NotLeaderOrFollower. Under acks=1, on SIGCONT the deposed leader appended and acked what the producer had buffered for 29 s, then learned of the new leader and truncated all of it. 8,731 acked records went. It wakes up still believing it leads, and the check that would tell it arrives after it has answered. The deposed leader cut its acks=all topic too, at offset 1633, but those records had never been acked.

The two logs after truncation agree at the fork, batch by batch (kafka-dump-log.sh --files on n2 and n3, columns trimmed):

The effect has a window. It needs a pause over the 9 s session, so leadership moves, and under the 30 s request timeout, so buffered sends are still live on waking. D64 files it as class D: a frozen leader is worse for acks=1 than a dead one. Upstream status was not checked.

A dead leader, in the same harness: SIGKILL under load lost nothing in this run at either level, because the surviving followers already held the acked records. The acks=1 zero is one sample, not a guarantee. It also delayed clients less. The longest ack gap was 8.9 s after SIGKILL against about 29 s frozen, even though the controller had moved leadership by the first sample after 18 s. A closed socket is news, and a silent one is not.

The SIGKILL run shows the mirror image of loss as well: under acks=all, 26,249 acked and 26,252 read back. An error at the client does not mean the record is absent.
