{{S}}The codecs that matter in 2026

The general-purpose lossless formats are old and fixed; their libraries are not. A level picks a point among ratio, compression speed and decompression speed. On Silesia, three settings make the frontier, zstd 1, zstd 9 and xz -9; zstd's default sits at its edge, and Brotli's and zlib's defaults sit off it. measured here

DEFLATE (RFC 1951) and its wrappers, zlib (RFC 1950) and gzip (RFC 1952), date from May 1996. from the spec With the format fixed, implementations compete on speed and parsing. zlib-ng's README says "x86-64 can be about 4x faster than stock zlib"; libdeflate runs levels to 12 for optimal parsing; Google's 2013 post on the compress-only Zopfli claims output "typically 3–8% smaller compared to zlib at maximum compression" for "2–3 orders of magnitude more" CPU time. from the docs

Brotli (RFC 7932, July 2016) adds context modelling and a built-in dictionary to LZ77 and Huffman coding. from the spec Its library and command line default to quality 11, the slowest; zstd's to level 3, zlib's to level 6. from the docs Default against default compares two efforts, not two formats. reconstruction The line that Brotli beats gzip by 20–26% swaps the baseline: Google's 2015 announcement said "20–26% higher compression ratios over Zopfli", and the Web Almanac 2025 repeats the figure against gzip, unsourced. from the docs On Silesia, brotli 11's output is 25.5% smaller than zlib -9's, and brotli 5's 12.7% smaller than zlib -6's. measured here For JSON, see JSON: pick a level, then stop.

RFC 8478 (October 2018) registered Zstandard's media type and content coding; RFC 8878 (February 2021), which replaced it, is "not an Internet Standards Track specification". from the spec v1.5.7 (19 February 2025) is still the newest release. Levels run 1 to 19, default 3; --ultra unlocks 20 to 22 and --fast negative levels. from the docs --long and dictionaries are in Saying it again, by reference.

LZ4 and Snappy each describe themselves as an LZ77-type compressor with a fixed, byte-oriented encoding and no entropy coder; Apple's LZFSE adds FSE. from the docs xz and 7-Zip ship LZMA, which xz decodes with a range decoder: in XZ Utils 5.8.4, src/liblzma/lzma/lzma_decoder.c line 17 includes range_decoder.h. from the docs The 2024 xz backdoor is in When compression is the attack. Meta's OpenZL (6 October 2025) builds a graph of transforms from a description of the data, decodes everything with one decoder, and falls back to zstd. On sao, a Silesia star catalogue, Meta reports 2.06× against 1.31× for zstd -3 and 1.64× for xz -9, on an Apple M1; on text, "When there is no structure, there is no advantage." from the docs Figure {{fig-codec-ledger}} collects them.

One corpus, twenty settings

Figure {{fig-frontier}} measures 20 settings of seven codecs. Each compresses the twelve Silesia files, 211,938,580 bytes, and is decoded and compared byte for byte, so ratios are exact. Speeds come from a 12 MiB sample, the first MiB of each file, timed on one thread (median of 7 runs; 3 for compressions over 3 s) on a shared 2-CPU machine, so they are tiers. measured here The installed libraries were zlib 1.3.1, libzstd 1.5.7, liblzma 5.8.3, libbz2 1.0.8, brotli 1.2.0 at window 22, liblz4 1.9.4 and cramjam's Rust Snappy. measured here

Ratios travel; speeds do not. lzbench 2.4 (27 September 2026, one thread of an AMD EPYC 9555P) prints compressed size as a percentage of the original, where the zstd and LZ4 READMEs print original ÷ compressed: its 34.53% for zstd -1 is their 2.896. from the docs Where lzbench lists the same setting, every codec here but Snappy lands within 0.015 percentage points of it. Snappy gives 48.193% against 47.85% for Google's C++ 1.3.1, for reasons not established. measured here

Read Figure {{fig-frontier}} by tier. At 100 MB/s or more the best ratio is zstd 1's 2.89×; from 10 to 100 MB/s, zstd 9's 3.59×; from 1 to 10, xz -9's 4.35×. Brotli 1 and 5 trail zstd 1 and 9 by under 1%, and brotli 11, alone below 1 MB/s, reaches 4.20×, short of xz -6. zlib -9 (3.13×) is off the frontier: zstd 3 gives 3.21× a tier faster, at about 100 MB/s. measured here Snappy's README claims an order of magnitude more speed than zlib's fastest mode, for output 20% to 100% bigger. from the docs Here it is a tier faster than zlib -1, and 32% bigger. measured here

Decompression is the cheap side; zstd's README says its speed "remains roughly the same at all settings". from the docs Here every zstd and zlib level decodes at 100 MB/s or more, while zstd's compression falls two tiers from level −5 to 22. The gap grows with effort: brotli 11 and zstd 19 decode hundreds of times faster than they encode, xz -9 some 30 times, and bzip2 -9 about twice, the slowest decoder here. measured here Arch Linux moved its packages from xz to zstd in December 2019 for "a ~1300% speedup" in decompression at "a total ~0.8% increase in package size". from the docs Here zstd 19's output is 8.4% larger than xz -9's and decodes a tier faster. measured here Brotli 11 suits compress-once text: slowest to encode, a tier faster than xz to decode, and asked for by browsers (Compression on the wire). reconstruction
