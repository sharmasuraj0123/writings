{{S}}Where the bet loses

The Markov property is an assumption, and assumptions have failure modes worth knowing by name. None of what follows argues against using these models. It argues for knowing which of your numbers survive the assumption being wrong — because some do, and the ones that do not tend to be exactly the numbers people page each other about.

1 · Real arrivals are not Poisson

The most consequential empirical result in this area is now more than thirty years old. Leland, Taqqu, Willinger and Wilson's 1994 study of Ethernet traffic at Bellcore showed that packet arrivals are self-similar: burstiness does not average out as you zoom out, it persists across timescales, and the traffic has long-range dependence rather than the exponentially decaying correlation a Markov model implies. from the literature Poisson arrivals smooth out when aggregated. Real traffic does not. The same has since been reported for web request streams, storage workloads and datacentre traffic.

What this breaks is specific: queue-length and buffer-occupancy predictions from M/M/· models are optimistic, sometimes by orders of magnitude, because the model expects bursts to cancel and they correlate instead. What it does not break is Little's law, or the qualitative shape of the utilisation curve in Figure {{fig-mm1}} — congestion still detonates near saturation, generally sooner.

2 · Heavy tails, at the same mean

Service times misbehave in a second, independent way. Memorylessness implies an exponential distribution, and exponentials have thin tails: the probability of a very long request decays geometrically. Measured latencies frequently do not. 3 · Failures are correlated

The redundancy arithmetic in Figure {{fig-availability}} assumes independent failures, and independence is what redundancy almost never buys. Replicas share a power feed, a network fabric, a configuration push, a container image, and the same bug reached by the same input. The computed 1 − (1 − A)² is therefore an upper bound on what two replicas deliver, and the gap is not small: incident histories are dominated by correlated causes, not by two independent machines failing to coincide. Use the formula to compare designs; do not put its output in a contract.

4 · Nothing is stationary

Time-homogeneity — the quiet second assumption from §1 — fails on every timescale that matters. Load is diurnal and weekly. Deploys change the service rate mid-measurement. Retry policies couple the arrival process to the service process, so a slowdown creates load, which is the mechanism behind metastable failures: a system that was stable at the same offered load ten minutes ago and will not recover when the load is removed. A single transition matrix cannot represent a system whose transition matrix is a function of its own recent behaviour.

5 · The state space explodes

Augmentation is always available and rarely affordable. The state space of a composed system is the product of its components' state spaces, which is the wall every model checker hits; symbolic representations, partial-order reduction and lumping (§5) push the wall back without removing it. The same bound is why exact POMDP solutions are unavailable and why tabular reinforcement learning does not scale. Whenever a Markov model of something real is tractable, someone has already thrown information away; the question is whether they told you which.

6 · You cannot tell when a chain has mixed

Restated from §7 because it belongs in this list: convergence is guaranteed and unverifiable. Diagnostics detect some failures and certify nothing, and a multimodal target can leave every diagnostic content while an entire mode goes unvisited. This is also the honest limit on simulation-based capacity estimates and on "we ran it until it looked stable".

What to do about all of it

A working rule, in three parts. Use Markov models for structure — what the states are, what transitions exist, where the feedback loops are, what the invariants must be. Trust their comparisons more than their absolute numbers: pooling really does beat partitioning, two bits of hysteresis really do beat one, damping really does control conditioning, and those conclusions survive the assumptions being approximate. And take tails from measurement, never from the model: report measured quantiles, cross-check with Little's law, which needs no distributional assumption at all, and check whether your holding times are actually exponential before believing any number derived from assuming they are. Markov models are excellent at telling you which way things move. They are unreliable about how far.
